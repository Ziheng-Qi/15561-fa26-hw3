Northwind Traders: where to focus next

A data review for the sales and operations manager: market growth, customer risk, discount policy and delivery reliability. 15.561 HW3.

1. Markets2. Key accounts 3. Discounts4. Delivery Method

Executive summary

Northwind Traders sold $1,265,793 across 830 orders from Jul 1996 to May 1998. Four things a manager should act on:

  1. Growth is strong but uneven. Jan-Apr revenue grew 2.2x year over year. Four small markets dipped, but an order-level check shows no broad decline: Denmark's dip is one large 1997 order, Canada lost one account, and Mexico and Finland are softening.
  2. Three customers bring in 25% of revenue, and two top-25 accounts have not ordered in 90+ days.
  3. Discounts above 5% buy no extra volume. Capping them could have kept about $58,483.
  4. Late deliveries come from tight deadlines, not shippers. 14-day orders are late 18% of the time and are not prioritized.

Question 1

Which customer markets are growing or shrinking, and where should sales attention go?

Why it matters

The company is expanding quickly. Overall growth can hide markets that are stalling. A shrinking market while the business doubles usually means lost accounts or a competitor, and it calls for a different response than a market that is simply small.

Approach

Net revenue is unit price × quantity × (1 − discount), summed by the customer's country. The data ends on May 06, 1998, so comparing calendar years would be unfair. I compare the same window, January 1 to April 30, in 1997 and 1998. Countries are ranked by dollar change, not percent, so tiny bases (Spain grew from $338) do not dominate. Small markets have only a few orders per window, so each shrinking market gets an order-level check: redo the comparison without its largest Jan-Apr 1997 order, compare the last six full months with the six before, and see whether that customer is still ordering.

Main finding

Jan-Apr revenue grew from $191,322 to $422,290 (2.2x). USA, Germany, Brazil supplied 57% of the dollar growth. 4 markets show lower Jan-Apr revenue: Denmark ($13,706 → $4,284), Canada ($15,211 → $11,526), Mexico ($6,965 → $3,885), Finland ($4,132 → $2,257). The order-level check shows none of them are a broad decline; in every case the drop disappears once the single largest 1997 order is removed. Finland: Softening (last 6 months -28%), no account lost. Mexico: Softening (last 6 months -40%), no account lost. Canada: One lost account (Mère Paillarde); rest of market growing. Business implication

Keep investing in the USA, Germany and Brazil, where growth is concentrated. Do not treat the shrinking markets as one problem. Denmark: no action beyond normal account care; the dip is one large order not repeated. Canada: a win-back call to Mère Paillarde (see Question 2). Finland, Mexico: small markets that are softening; check in with the main customers, but this is not yet a loss. France is a large market with flat sales, so it is the best place to test a growth push. Lesson for the dashboard: in small markets, a year-over-year drop should always be checked order by order before anyone acts on it.

Question 2

How dependent is revenue on a few customers, and are any key accounts going quiet?

Why it matters

If a handful of accounts drive the business, losing one is a material risk, and those accounts deserve dedicated account management. Spotting a big customer that has stopped ordering early gives sales a chance to win it back.

Approach

Lifetime net revenue per customer, sorted into a Pareto curve. For recency, I flag customers with no order in the last 90 days before the data ends. The median gap between a customer's orders is 35 days and 75% of gaps are under 76 days, so 90 days is unusually long. I focus on the top quarter of customers by revenue.

A simple monthly "top customers with no order in 90 days" report, as built here, would catch the next case automatically.

Question 3

Are discounts buying extra volume, or just giving away margin?

Why it matters

Discounts cost real money. If deeper discounts do not lead to bigger orders, the company can tighten its discount policy and keep the difference with little risk to volume.

Approach

I compare units per order line across the standard discount tiers (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25%). 8 lines with one-off discounts (1-6%) are excluded from the tier view. To control for product mix, I also compare each product's average quantity with and without a discount. Discount cost = list-price revenue minus net revenue. This is observational data, so differences are associations, not proof of cause.

Main finding

Discounts cost $88,666 (6.5% of list-price revenue) across 39% of order lines. Discounted lines are larger (mean 27.4 vs 21.7 units), and 70% of products sell more units per line when discounted. Deeper discounts, however, buy nothing extra: lines at 25% average 28.2 units, the same as lines at 5% (28.0; Mann-Whitney p = 0.31). Tiers above 5% account for $80,884 of the discount cost.

Business implication

Some discount seems to help: discounted lines are bigger. But 15-25% discounts show no volume gain over 5%. If those lines had been given 5% at the same quantity, the company would have kept about $58,483, which is roughly 4.6% of total revenue. Recommendation: make 5% the default, require manager approval above 10%, and run a short A/B-style pilot with a few accounts to confirm volume holds.

Question 4

Where do late deliveries come from: the shipper, the sales rep, or the deadline?

Why it matters

Late deliveries hurt customer retention, especially for wholesale food customers restocking shelves. Fixing them requires knowing the cause. Switching shippers will not help if the problem is internal.

Approach

An order is late if it shipped after its required date (the only delivery commitment in the data). 21 orders placed after early April 1998 had not shipped by the end of the data and are excluded; none were already past due. I compare late rates by shipper, sales rep, and the time the customer allowed (order date to required date, which is always 14, 28 or 42 days), and use Fisher's exact and chi-square tests to check whether gaps exceed chance.

Main finding

Overall 4.6% of shipped orders were late (37 of 809). Shippers barely differ (3.6% to 5.1%; chi-square p = 0.68). The real driver is the deadline: orders with only 14 days allowed are late 18% of the time vs 3.7% for the standard 28 days (Fisher p < 0.001), and they account for 32% of all late orders while being only 68 orders. Worse, these rush orders are not handled faster. They take 10.0 days to ship on average vs 8.3 for standard orders (p = 0.06).

Business implication

Do not renegotiate shipping contracts over lateness; the shippers perform about the same. Instead, flag 14-day orders in the warehouse and ship them first. Today they wait in the same queue as everything else. Rep-level differences are based on few late orders (1-10 each). Treat them as a coaching conversation, not a performance verdict.

Data and method notes

  • Source: the course-provided Northwind workbook (Orders, Order Details, Customers, Products, Categories, Employees, Shippers, Suppliers).

  • Discounts are stored as 32-bit floats (5% is read as 0.0500000007), so they are rounded to two decimals before grouping.
  • Data ends May 06, 1998. Year-over-year comparisons use Jan-Apr only.
  • All results are associations in historical data. The discount and delivery recommendations should be confirmed with a small pilot.
